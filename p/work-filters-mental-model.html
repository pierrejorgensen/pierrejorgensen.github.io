Filtering and the mental model

The job Product listing (PLP) filters aren't performing.
The industry Bridal gowns. The company is a global leader in the wedding-gowns industry. Its sites serve both consumer and wholesaler audiences — brides and bridal boutiques.
The business goal Increase engagement with the PLP.
My role Two functional hats: Product manager and UX designer. My team is only just learning the ropes of product ownership and management, and we have no dedicated designer, so delegating is not an option.
Before: One large, prominent “Filter & Sort ” opens a conventional filter drawer.

Engagement with the product filters is low. Visitors land on a PLP, may scroll partway down, then navigate to a different gallery. The prominent “Filter & Sort” unused.

Why is that important? Engagement is a proxy for likelihood of purchase. Essense does not sell online. The objective is for the potential customer — the bride — to attach to a specific dress or the brand, then seek out a bridal boutique that carries the brand. The more the bride actively engages with the product selection, as opposed to simply browsing, the more likely that attachment is.

Current state

The conventional e-commerce patters is familiar: You open some sort of drawer or side panel with facets organized in groups of attributes. That's what we have here, so why aren't people using it?

The button next to it, “Change Model”, is unclear, which surely doesn't help (it actually changes the default product shot from a an interior or exterior “scene” setting to an studio shot against a neutral background). That shouldn't be enough to cause visitors to avoid the filters altogether.

Typically, when functionality is underused, look for root cause first. If you pour all your efforts into optimizing the trigger mechanism, your call to action button or link, you are likely going to tinker around the margins. My money is on something for fundamental: The functionality doesn't solve a problem the visitor/user actually has, or it doesn't solve it in way that's useful for them.

Hypothesis: It's the mental model

My hunch is, brides aren't browsing dresses the way you'd shop for, say, a refrigerator. You don't have a familiar set of product attributes to filter by, like dimensions, side-by-side vs. bottom freezer, white vs. stainless steel front. You are likely not to even quite know what style of dress you want, or even if you do what that style is called.

We're looking at a circular, exploratory model, not linear “let's narrow this down” filtering. The site is set up for something different from what you actually need.

With no design system (yet), visual design needed to include some exploration and variations.

The solution

The answer is to make trial-and-error exploration easy and obvious, which means exposing the facets and letting you combine and recombine them in real time.

Constraint: We don't want to take up permanent space down the side of the page on desktop, and on mobile this has to be compact. Browsing wedding gowns is still very much a visual “I know it when I see it” exercise, so we do not want to constrain the width of the product photography in any way. A ribbon across the top will do the trick. It won't occupy more space than the current fat buttons already take up.

(Side note: Exploring the visual presentation of the ribbon was more of a visual exercise, and took more time, than a feature like this would normally need. That was my choice. Why? Because Essense did not yet have a design system. UI controls were all over the place, from squared off to completely rounded and with no consistent dimensions, borders, or background colors. I needed to draw a line in the sand and start a new set of standards; the visual language we chose for this ribbon would be a new baseline to build from, so it was important to get buy-in and consensus.)
