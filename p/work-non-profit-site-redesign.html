Conversion-funnel design for a non-profit

The job Redesign a poorly converting site's architecture.
The industry Non-profit child sponsorship. Children International finances programs for poor children in several countries mostly with sponsorship donations, analogous to an e-commerce subscription model.
The business strategy Increase donations by converting more site visits to sponsors.
My role Information architect, responsible for laying out the conversion funnel in the form of navigation structure and rough page layouts, with deliverables in the form of wires, rough sketches, and user journey maps.

Background

A Kansas City-based charity desperately needed a ground-up redesign of its website. I came in to draw up the blueprints for a new user experience.

Children International (CI) finances its programs for poor children in several countries mostly with sponsorship donations, so the single most important goal for the redesign was obvious: Increase donations by adding more, new sponsors.

The first order of business is to agree on what the current problems are so you can fix them. There were multiple, not least of which was an overall lack of focus and direction on the current site, but one was glaring. If you want to sponsor a child, CI makes you create an account first.

Removing the account wall

The solution is deceptively simple. Any checkout form collects exactly the information required for an account, so there is no reason to create an account and then take payment. All we need to do is create an account when the visitor submits payment.

Of course, there are what-ifs: What if the visitor already has an account? Add log-in fields for current customers. Logging in will pre-fill all the information we already have. Better yet, use AJAX to do this without reloading the page.

What if someone has an account but doesn’t log in, won’t we create a duplicate account? No. On the back end, simply check for an existing account with the e-mail address and name. If one exists, use it and don’t create a new one.

Funnels

Once we had goals defined, and we knew the problems we were trying to solve, I introduced the concept of funnels. The idea is simple: Build the site around, and measure, what you need people to do. If you sell hot sauce, you need them to buy hot sauce, or, e.g., submit a recipe. If you solicit donations, you need people to donate.

A funnel is the flow that leads your visitor to that goal. It tells you which paths through the site are important, and it tells you what to measure.

The obvious funnel for CI ends in signing up for a new sponsorship. Others included generating a hot lead (a call or e-mail from a high-value donor) to one of their fundraisers; submitting a testimonial; and requesting a free information kit.

Site map, navigation, and home page

The current site suffered from, in technical terms, too much stuff. Cognitive psychologists will tell you that more choice doesn’t make you more satisfied, it tires you out. Instant access to everything all at once is good for a library but not a site that should be supporting a mission. At CI’s site, you’d arrive and be bombarded with choices competing for attention. My solution was to draw up a dead simple site map and navigation. See the main navigation below? The focus is crystal clear: Please sponsor. That’s why we’re all here.

I intended for the home page to become the template for other landing pages in general, so it was important to get it right. We needed to include just the right elements for persuasion, and we needed to design a clear eye path.

The mock-up below shows the basic structure of the home/landing pages. I designed it around an initial hook-shaped eye path ending in the call to action and child search function. You can never be sure you get the eye path right until you test it with heat maps, but you can make an educated guess. When you’re replacing a design that’s known not to work, you’re better off launching a good, educated guess and then tweaking it based on data from A/B and heat map testing. If you spend months agonizing over the exact layout instead, the current site is sitting there losing money in the meantime. In business terms, that’s your opportunity cost.

Design by Sharpie

To lay out sub-pages, instead of working on screen I opted to use “Design by Sharpie.” (Yes, I did just make that up. Feel free to use it.) I got three people from stakeholder groups and myself together in a room for two hours at a time, twice a week, to plow through the most important pages. I didn’t want a committee, and I didn’t want endless meetings; I wanted a working group. So, we used pen and paper. Why? Because when you use a mock-up tool, Photoshop, or even HTML to mock up pages, the irresistible temptation is to start embellishing the minutiae. You lose sight of the forest and start coloring the trees. Most review processes encourage this, because as soon as you show stakeholders a Photoshop mock-up you get bogged down in discussions about colors, fonts, and the size of photos.

When you put a Sharpie to paper you remove the option of moving boxes a few pixels back and forth, and you won’t waste hours arguing the color of buttons. Pen on paper is a way to get the bones of your pages right, quickly and with a minimum of fuss. It frees you to focus on what needs to go on the page and where. You’re focusing on what the page needs to do before you consider what it needs to look like.

The remarks in red are for the developers. With paper mock-ups like this, you can march straight over to the developers and give them a heads up that this is what’s going in the requirements. They immediately know what it means and how we want it to work, so those red scribbles on a piece of paper are more useful than a sentence like “The system shall provide advanced search results without reloading the page,” buried in a 98-page requirements document.

Child search and short list

The process of searching and picking children to sponsor could be frustrating. You’d search, browse the results, and see a child’s detail. Unless you were convinced, and most people would look at several children before they select one, you would go back to the results, keep paging, and repeat the process. Each time you see a child’s detail, you leave the search results. You had to remember which children you’d already seen. Often, people would have one child in mind but still want to browse. How would they remember that child after they’d scrolled through several more pages of search results?

I recommended we make heavy use of AJAX to keep it all on one page. Advanced search should update the results in real time without a reload, and child details should appear on an in-page panel (a “modal dialog” or “jQuery window”). The experience would be much more fluid and seamless without the constant page reloads.

Better yet, we needed a mechanism for people to save a list of “maybes” — children you might sponsor and want to come back to unless someone else grabs your attention. I did not want to use a cart, because I did not want to portray the process as shopping for children. Instead, I settled on a short list. You would add a child to your short list, keep browsing, and not have to worry about remembering the details of that child.

Checkout

I wanted to keep the checkout process dead simple. Most transactions require only enough information to take payment. There is no reason to collect information like birthday or phone number. If you want to make your donation in honor of or name of someone else, that information can be entered on a second page. The objective is to put as few barriers in place for you to complete what you wanted to do: Make your payment to start sponsoring.

Using AJAX to validate the form in real time is a better user experience not just because it feels slicker and more professional (it does), but because it eliminates the flicker and page jumping you get with a reload.
