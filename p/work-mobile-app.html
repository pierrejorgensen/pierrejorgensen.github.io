G2C (government-to-consumer) app

The job Design the mobile and Web apps for a new line of business from scratch.
The industry NIC, Inc. was a SaaS provider serving state and local government with payment processing and UI, invoice management, Web portals, and other digital solutions.
The business strategy NIC had operated as an intermediary but wanted to open a direct line to citizens. The initiative was to turn that model inside out and connect directly to citizens: Run our own app where you could manage and pay property taxes, vehicle registration and driver license renewals, hunting and fishing licenses, and national and state park passes.
My role Lead designer owning everything from information architecture to production-ready UI.

Background

NIC wants to launch an app to simplify your interactions with state and local government: Pay your property taxes, get reminders when and where to vote, buy and renew licenses and permits, and so on.

We already had an inventory of tasks and information we’d initially be able to offer. My first order of business was to map out a natural interaction flow for each of the various tasks.

Information architecture and interaction flows

These flows together became the road map.

Design: Navigation

Next, my goal was to organize the information and tasks into a navigation scheme that would feel natural for end users. This was not as obvious as it might seem. NIC’s core business is to serve government agencies, which means adopting their naming conventions and process flows. We would have to turn that model inside out, and I had no prior personas or user research to work with.

To solve that problem, I used online card-sorting services and ran two rounds of testing with a few hundred participants. We came up with a relatively simple structure: The government services we would initially serve could all roll up into Cars and Property, Voting, and Outdoors. (Business services such as licensing and permit, and interactions with the court systems beyond traffic-related fines, were out of scope.)

Design: Sign-up

We were able to keep setup simple. The app requires nothing more than the user’s e-mail address and Zip code, the latter to localize for state and county.

Setting up the app requires no more than e-mail and location, but it has no utility until you add information. The app encourages you to do so when you first set it up in a series of guided steps to minimize friction.

Design: Payment screen

Once the app is set up, its primary utility is informational. To make it truly useful, you’ll want to also make payments. Today, you’ll pay, e.g., the DMV and the county assessor separately. Want to buy a fishing permit, pay a parking ticket, or reserve a shelter at a local park? Three separate agencies, three separate Web sites, three separate payments.

NIC’s core business is payment processing and UI for government agencies, which means it is already integrated with thousands of state and local governments’ and utilities’ invoicing and payment systems. Having one or more saved payment methods in Gov2Go makes it not just another online wallet but a clearinghouse to all kinds of payments and bills you’d otherwise have to manage separately.

Taking payments and storing payment information, of course, requires an additional layer of security. Where initial setup of the app requires nothing besides publicly available information, which allowed us to skip the speed bump of having users create a password initially, now we need one, which means the dreaded password rules.

Rather than use the common model of describing the requirements and then correcting users with error messages when those rules are not met, I wanted to have the system give instant, positive feedback.
